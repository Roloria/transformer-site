    First, what is this thing?

    A Transformer is a machine that reads text and writes text. You give it some words; it thinks for a moment; it gives you back some words.

    The interesting part is how it thinks. It does not read left-to-right, one word at a time, the way you do. Instead, it reads the whole sentence all at once, and for every word it asks a simple question:

    “Which other words in this sentence should I pay attention to?”

    It writes down the answer, re-writes every word using what it learned from the words it looked at, and then asks the same question again — but now with the richer version of each word. It does this dozens of times. By the end, every word knows about every other word.

    That's the whole trick. Read everything, all the time. Everything else — the math, the diagrams, the long words — is just a careful way to make that one idea work in practice.

    Three sentences to remember

    1. Input. A piece of text, broken into chunks called tokens.
    2. Process. Each token reads every other token, repeatedly, and slowly rewrites itself with what it learned.
    3. Output. A new piece of text — the next word, a translation, a summary, an answer.
    You type
    “Once upon a”
    →
    The model reads it, many times
    →
    You get
    “time…”

    That's it. Everything below is just unpacking how those three boxes work.

    Now see why it mattered.

    Before 2017, language models read like a slow typist. See how the Transformer changed that.

    Continue → Premise
    In plain English

    Old models read like a slow typist: one keystroke at a time, trying to remember what they just saw in a single mental sticky note. By the time they reached the end of a long sentence, the beginning had faded. Transformers read the whole page at once and write little notes about who relates to whom. Much faster, much harder to forget.

    01 · Premise
    4 min readbeginner

    Why the Transformer won.

    Before 2017, sequence modeling meant recurrence. RNNs and LSTMs read tokens one at a time, hiding a memory in a hidden state that grew stale with distance. The Transformer threw that out — and replaced it with a different idea: let every token look at every other token, directly, in one step.

    Before

    RNN / LSTM

    • Sequential. Token n depends on token n−1.
    • Vanishing signal. Long-range dependencies decay.
    • Hard to parallelize. GPUs sit idle most of the time.
    • Hidden bottleneck. One vector carries everything.
    After

    Transformer

    • Parallel. Every token is processed simultaneously.
    • Direct routing. Any token can address any other in O(1) hops.
    • GPU-friendly. Just big matrix multiplies.
    • Content-based addressing. Learned queries & keys.

    Up next · Section 5

    The heart: self-attention.

    Every token asks "who else here is relevant to me?" and rewrites itself from the answers.

    Continue → Attention
    In plain English

    Imagine six people standing in a row, each holding a card with one word. Every person looks at every other person and decides how related they are. Then each person rewrites their card as a blend of everyone else's, weighted by how related they decided to be. That's it. The math below is just a careful way to do "look at everyone, blend by relevance."

    Isn't this just looking up the closest word?

    No. Attention doesn't pick one word — it takes a weighted average of all words. Every output is a mixture, not a lookup. The weights are smooth, fractional, and learned from data.

    04 · Self-Attention
    8 min readcore

    The heart of it all: scaled dot-product attention.

    Every token emits three vectors — a Query, a Key, and a Value. A query asks a question. Every key says how well it answers. The values are blended in proportion to those answers.

    The formula
    $$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$
    Query — what I'm looking for Key — what I contain Value — what I actually contribute

    Step 1 · Project into Q, K, V

    Three learned linear projections turn each token's embedding into three roles.

    Step 2 · Score every pair: QKᵀ

    For each query position, compute a dot product with every key position. Large dot product → those tokens are related.

    Step 3 · Scale by √dk, then softmax

    Dividing by √dk keeps gradients well-behaved when dk is large. Softmax turns scores into a probability distribution over keys.

    scores = QKᵀ scaled = scores / √dk weights = softmax(scaled) output = weights · V

    Many heads, many insights.

    Eight attentions in parallel, on smaller slices. Each one learns to track something different.

    Continue → Multihead
    In plain English

    Imagine eight experts in a meeting, all reading the same sentence. One tracks who-does-what. One tracks which pronouns refer to whom. One just looks at the word right before. Each writes their own notes, then the notes are stitched together. Different heads learn to look for different things — you don't have to tell them what to look for.

    06 · Multi-Head Attention
    4 min readcore

    Many heads see different things.

    Instead of one big attention, we run h attentions in parallel on smaller slices of the embedding, then concatenate. Different heads specialize: one tracks syntax, another tracks coreference, another just looks at the previous token.

    Multi-head formulation
    $$\text{MultiHead}(Q,K,V) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1,\dots,\text{head}_h)\,W^O$$ $$\text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(QW_i^Q,\, KW_i^K,\, VW_i^V)$$
    Up next · Section 8

    Where am I in the sentence?

    Attention is a bag of words. Positional encoding gives every position a unique fingerprint.

    Continue → Positions
    In plain English

    Attention treats a sentence like a bag of words — it sees "cat sat mat on the" exactly the same as "mat sat cat on the". To fix that, we add a position tag to every word. Think of it like numbering the cards 1, 2, 3… so the model knows which word came first. The clever part is that we don't just number them — we use a unique fingerprint for every position so the model can also measure how far apart two words are.

    Why not just say "this is word number 5"?

    Because plain numbers don't generalize: a model trained on sentences of length 20 has no idea what to do with a 100-word sentence. Sinusoidal (and rotary) embeddings give every position a unique fingerprint that also encodes relative distance — so position 50 and 100 still look "twice as far apart" as 25 and 50.

    07 · Positional Encoding
    4 min readcore

    Where are you in the sequence?

    Attention is permutation-equivariant: shuffle the inputs and the outputs shuffle the same way. That's a feature and a bug — language has order. We fix it by adding a deterministic position-dependent vector to every embedding.

    Sinusoidal (original paper)
    $$PE_{(pos,\,2i)} = \sin\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d}}\right) \quad\quad PE_{(pos,\,2i+1)} = \cos\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d}}\right)$$
    Each dimension is a sine at a different frequency. The model can recover the offset between any two positions via a linear combination — and unseen lengths extrapolate gracefully.
    position 0···position 100

    Modern transformers (GPT, LLaMA) use rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) — rotate Q and K by a position-dependent angle, so attention scores naturally depend on relative distance.

    08 · Feed-Forward

    The quiet half of the block.

    After attention mixes information across positions, the feed-forward network mixes information within each position. It's just two linear layers with a non-linearity between them — applied independently to every token.

    x
    →
    Lineard → 4d
    ReLUmax(0, ·)
    Linear4d → d
    →
    y
    Two-thirds of the parameters live here. In a standard Transformer block, the FFN is where the model stores most of its "factual" knowledge — attention routes information, the FFN processes it.
    In plain English

    Every time the model rewrites a word, it keeps a copy of the original and adds the changes on top — like saving your work in a video game. This way, if a layer doesn't know what to do, it can just output zero and leave the word unchanged. It also lets information flow freely backward during training, so very deep models stay trainable.

    Why does adding the input back help?

    Two reasons. Training: gradients can flow straight through the skip connection without getting smaller — essential when you have 96 layers stacked. Meaning: the sub-layer only has to learn the change (the "delta") from the input, which is often closer to zero than the full output.

    09 · Residual + LayerNorm

    Three flavors of model.

    Encoder, decoder, or both? Each architecture has different strengths.

    10 · Encoder vs. Decoder
    4 min readcore
    Encoder

    Reads the whole input, both directions.

    Bidirectional self-attention. Each token sees every other token in the same sequence. Best for understanding tasks: classification, embeddings, span prediction.

    BERT · RoBERTa · Sentence Transformers
    Decoder

    Writes one token at a time, looking back.

    Masked self-attention. Position i can only attend to positions ≤ i. Add cross-attention layers that pull K, V from the encoder. Best for generation: translation, chat, code.

    GPT · LLaMA · Claude · Mistral
    Encoder–Decoder

    Translate, summarize, infill.

    Full original Transformer. The encoder builds a representation of the source; the decoder generates the target one token at a time, cross-attending to the source at every step.

    T5 · BART · FLAN-T5 · Whisper

    11 · A Worked Example
    8 min readhands-on

    Let's trace one sentence, step by step.

    Take the sentence “The cat sat.” and follow it through every stage of the model — with actual numbers at each step. To keep the arithmetic visible, we'll pretend dmodel = 4 and dk = 2 (real models use hundreds).

    1

    Tokenize the sentence

    A tokenizer chops the input into pieces from a fixed vocabulary (about 50,000 entries). Common words are one piece; rare ones are split.

    “The cat sat.” → Thecatsat.

    Each piece becomes an integer ID from the vocabulary:
    1012,3711,42313
    2

    Look up an embedding for each token

    Every token ID maps to a learned vector — a row in a giant table. The vector is what “means” looks like to the model.

    The → [ +0.20, +0.80, −0.50, +0.30 ] cat → [ −0.40, +0.90, +0.10, −0.20 ] sat → [ +0.30, +0.10, +0.60, +0.40 ] . → [ +0.05, −0.05, +0.02, +0.01 ]
    3

    Add position information

    Without this step the model can't tell “cat sat mat” from “mat sat cat”. We add a unique fingerprint for each position.

    Position 1: [ 0.00, +0.10, 0.00, +0.10 ] Position 2: [ 0.00, +0.20, 0.00, +0.20 ] Position 3: [ 0.00, +0.30, 0.00, +0.30 ] Position 4: [ 0.00, +0.40, 0.00, +0.40 ]
    The + pos1 = [ +0.20, +0.90, −0.50, +0.40 ]
    4

    Project each token into Q, K, and V

    Three learned matrices (WQ, WK, WV) turn each token's vector into three smaller “voices”. Each is dk = 2 numbers long.

    Q(The) = [ +0.20, +0.90 ] Q(cat) = [−0.40, +0.90 ] K(The) = [−0.50, +0.40 ] K(cat) = [ +0.10, −0.20 ] V(The) = [ +1.00, −0.20 ] V(cat) = [ +0.50, −0.10 ]
    5

    Score every pair: Q · Kᵀ

    For each query position i, dot-product with every key position j. Big dot product → those tokens are related.

    Example: how related is cat to each token?

    score(cat, The) = (−0.40)(−0.50) + (+0.90)(+0.40) = +0.56 score(cat, cat) = (−0.40)(+0.10) + (+0.90)(−0.20) = −0.22 score(cat, sat) = (−0.40)(... ) + (+0.90)(... ) = −0.08 After dividing by √dk = √2 ≈ 1.414:   = [+0.40, −0.16, −0.06]
    6

    Softmax turns scores into weights

    Softmax squashes the three numbers so they sum to 1 and every weight is non-negative. Now they are probabilities over positions.

    raw: [ +0.40, −0.16, −0.06 ] exp: [ 1.49, 0.85, 0.94 ] sum: 3.28 weights:  [ 0.45, 0.26, 0.29 ]
    Read aloud: when thinking about “cat”, pay 45% attention to “The”, 26% to “cat”, 29% to “sat”.
    7

    Weighted sum of values — done

    Multiply each token's value vector by its weight and add them up. The result is the new representation of “cat” — now informed by its neighbors.

    output(cat) = 0.45 · V(The) + 0.26 · V(cat) + 0.29 · V(sat) = 0.45·[+1.00,−0.20] + 0.26·[+0.50,−0.10] + 0.29·[+0.90,+0.50]          = [ +0.86, +0.06 ]
    ↻

    Now do this n times

    In a real model this whole pipeline runs 6 to 96 times, with fresh Q/K/V matrices each round. After all those rounds, each token's vector encodes information from the entire sentence — and that rich representation is what the model uses to predict the next word, classify a sentence, translate, or anything else.

    14 · Glossary
    referencereference

    Every term, in one place.

    Bookmark this. Any time a word below shows up in a paper or video and you forgot what it meant, come back here.

    Token

    A piece of text from a fixed vocabulary — usually a word, part of a word, or punctuation. The model never sees characters; it sees tokens.

    Embedding

    The vector of numbers that represents a token. Each token has one row in a giant learned table.

    dmodel

    The width of every vector inside the model. Original paper: 512. Modern models: 4096 to 16384.

    Query (Q)

    The “question” a token asks. “Who here is relevant to me?”

    Key (K)

    The “answer label” a token carries. “Here is what I am about.”

    Value (V)

    The “information” a token contributes if selected. “If you decide I'm relevant, here's what to take from me.”

    Attention score

    A single number for every pair of positions, computed as Q · K. Higher means “more related”.

    Softmax

    A function that turns any list of numbers into a probability distribution — every output is in (0, 1) and they sum to 1.

    Scaled dot-product

    The full formula: softmax(QKᵀ / √dk) V. The √dk keeps the numbers from getting too large.

    Head

    One parallel attention computation. A model with 8 heads runs 8 attentions at once, on different slices of the embedding.

    Positional encoding

    A vector added to each token's embedding so the model knows where it sits in the sequence.

    Residual connection

    Adding the input of a layer back to its output: output = x + Sublayer(x). Lets gradients flow and lets layers learn small adjustments.

    LayerNorm

    A normalization that rescales every vector to mean 0, variance 1. Keeps activations well-behaved at every depth.

    FFN (Feed-Forward Network)

    Two linear layers with a non-linearity between, applied independently to every token. Most of the model's parameters live here.

    Encoder

    A stack of layers that reads a full sequence with bidirectional attention. Used for understanding tasks.

    Decoder

    A stack of layers that generates a sequence one token at a time, with masked (causal) attention. Used for generation.

    Causal mask

    A triangular mask that prevents position i from attending to positions > i. Required for autoregressive generation.

    Cross-attention

    Attention where Q comes from the decoder but K and V come from the encoder. The bridge between the two stacks.

    Autoregressive

    Generating output one token at a time, feeding each prediction back into the model as input for the next.

    Logits

    Raw scores for every possible next token, right before the final softmax. Larger = the model thinks it's more likely.
